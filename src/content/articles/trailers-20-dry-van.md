A 20-foot dry van is a short, enclosed trailer used for hauling freight that doesn't need temperature control. It's a niche option compared to the standard 53-foot van, but it has specific advantages for certain loads and routes. This guide covers what it is, what it's used for, what it costs, and how to decide if one fits your business.

## What Is a 20' Dry Van?

A 20-foot dry van is a box trailer with a cargo floor length of 20 feet. It's typically 8 feet wide and 8 to 9 feet tall, giving roughly 1,200 to 1,400 cubic feet of cargo space. It has a rear roll-up or swing door, and some models have a side door for easier loading. It's designed to be pulled by a standard tractor, but it's also common with straight trucks or small fleets.

Key specs vary by manufacturer, but here are typical numbers:

| Spec | Typical Range |
|------|---------------|
| Length | 20 ft (floor) |
| Width | 96 to 102 in |
| Height | 8 to 9 ft |
| Cubic capacity | 1,200 to 1,400 cu ft |
| GVWR | 10,000 to 14,000 lbs |
| Payload | 6,000 to 10,000 lbs |

## Common Uses for a 20' Dry Van

A 20-foot dry van is not a general freight workhorse. It's used for specific jobs where a full-size trailer is overkill. Common applications include:

- **Local deliveries**: Moving appliances, furniture, or building materials to job sites or retail stores where space is tight.
- **Small parcel freight**: Handling LTL (less-than-truckload) shipments that don't fill a 53-footer.
- **Expedited freight**: When you need to move a small, high-value load fast, a 20-footer is easier to maneuver and often faster to load/unload.
- **Urban routes**: Navigating narrow streets, alleys, and tight loading docks is easier with a shorter trailer.
- **Specialized equipment**: Some companies use 20-foot vans for mobile workshops, storage, or to haul equipment like generators or compressors.

## Cost of a 20' Dry Van: Purchase and Operating

If you're thinking about buying or leasing, here's what you can expect in 2026. Prices vary by manufacturer, condition, and features.

### Purchase Price

- **New**: A new 20-foot dry van from a major manufacturer like Great Dane, Wabash, or Utility typically costs **$18,000 to $28,000**. That's for a basic model. Add $2,000 to $5,000 for options like a side door, liftgate, or special flooring.
- **Used**: A used 20-footer in good condition runs **$8,000 to $15,000**, depending on age and wear. You can find cheaper units, but inspect them carefully for rust, floor damage, and roof leaks.

### Leasing and Rental

- **Lease**: Monthly lease rates for a 20-foot dry van are usually **$300 to $500 per month** on a 3 to 5 year term.
- **Rental**: Daily rental from companies like Ryder or Penske runs **$50 to $100 per day**, plus mileage. Weekly rates are often $250 to $500.

### Operating Costs

- **Insurance**: Adding a trailer to your policy typically costs **$200 to $500 per year** for liability and physical damage.
- **Maintenance**: Expect **$500 to $1,000 per year** for routine maintenance like brakes, lights, and tire replacement. Tires for a 20-footer cost about **$150 to $250 each**.
- **Licensing and registration**: Annual registration varies by state, but budget **$100 to $300**.

## Pros and Cons of a 20' Dry Van

### Pros

- **Maneuverability**: Easier to back into tight spots and navigate city streets.
- **Lower cost**: Cheaper to buy, insure, and maintain than a 53-footer.
- **Less dead weight**: You're not hauling a 15,000-pound trailer when you only need 5,000 pounds of capacity.
- **Faster loading**: Smaller space means less time to load and unload, which can improve turnaround.

### Cons

- **Limited capacity**: You can't haul large or bulky loads. If a customer needs 30 pallets, you'll need multiple trips.
- **Less efficient for long hauls**: Per mile, a 20-footer costs about the same as a 53-footer to operate, but you're moving less freight. That cuts into profit.
- **Resale value**: The market for used 20-foot vans is thinner, so resale may take longer.

## How to Decide If a 20' Dry Van Is Right for You

Ask yourself these questions before you commit:

1. **What freight do you haul?** If your typical load is under 10,000 pounds and doesn't fill a 53-footer, a 20-footer might work.
2. **Where do you run?** If you're mostly local or regional with tight delivery points, the shorter length helps.
3. **What's your budget?** A 20-footer is cheaper upfront, but you need to calculate if the lower capacity still lets you make money.
4. **Do you have a dedicated lane?** If you have a contract that specifies small loads, a 20-footer makes sense. If you rely on spot market freight, a 53-footer is more flexible.

## Practical Steps to Take This Week

If you're seriously considering a 20-foot dry van, here's what to do now:

- **Measure your typical loads**: Look at your last 10 shipments. Note the weight and cubic footage. If most are under 1,200 cubic feet, a 20-footer could work.
- **Get insurance quotes**: Call two or three commercial insurance agents and ask for a quote on adding a 20-foot trailer to your policy. That gives you a real cost.
- **Check local availability**: Search online for new and used 20-foot dry vans in your area. See what's available and at what price.
- **Run the numbers**: Use a simple spreadsheet to compare your current trailer's costs and revenue against a 20-footer. Include purchase, insurance, maintenance, and fuel. Be honest about how many loads you'd actually run.

## FAQ

### Can a 20-foot dry van be pulled by a pickup truck?

Yes, if the pickup has a proper fifth-wheel hitch and enough payload capacity. A 20-foot trailer with a GVWR of 14,000 pounds requires a truck rated to tow at least that, like a heavy-duty 3/4-ton or 1-ton pickup. Check your truck's towing specs before you buy.

### How many pallets fit in a 20-foot dry van?

With standard 48x40-inch pallets, you can fit about 10 pallets if you load them straight in. If you use a side door and load them sideways, you might get 12, but it's tight. Always measure your pallets and the trailer's interior width.

### Is a 20-foot dry van worth it for a new owner-operator?

It depends on your business model. If you have a niche like expedited or local delivery, it can be a low-cost entry. But if you plan to use load boards for general freight, a 53-footer is more practical because most loads expect that size.

### What's the difference between a 20-foot dry van and a 20-foot reefer?

A dry van has no temperature control. A reefer has a refrigeration unit and insulated walls, so it can haul perishable goods. Reefers cost more to buy and maintain, and they're heavier. If you don't need temperature control, stick with a dry van.

## The Bottom Line

A 20-foot dry van is a specialized tool, not a general-purpose trailer. It shines for local deliveries, small loads, and tight spaces, but it won't replace a 53-footer for long-haul freight. If you have a steady stream of small loads and want to cut costs, it's worth a serious look. But run the numbers first, and don't buy one just because it's cheaper. Match the trailer to the work you actually do.
