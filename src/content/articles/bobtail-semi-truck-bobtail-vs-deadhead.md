If you're new to trucking, you've probably heard the terms bobtail and deadhead thrown around. Both mean driving without a trailer, but they're not the same thing. Bobtailing is when you drive the tractor alone, usually to pick up a trailer or for maintenance. Deadheading is when you pull an empty trailer to your next load. Knowing the difference matters for your safety, your wallet, and your compliance.

## What Is Bobtailing?

Bobtailing means operating a semi truck without a trailer attached. The tractor has no trailer, so the rear axle is unloaded. You might bobtail to:

- Pick up a trailer at a shipper or yard
- Go to a repair shop for service
- Move the truck between terminals
- Take the truck home for the weekend

Bobtailing is common, but it changes how the truck handles. Without a trailer, the front axle carries more weight, and the rear wheels have less traction. Braking is different, and the truck can fishtail more easily, especially on wet or icy roads.

## What Is Deadheading?

Deadheading means pulling an empty trailer to a pickup location. You're still driving a full combination, but the trailer has no cargo. Deadheading often happens when:

- You drop off a load and your next pickup is far away
- You reposition to a better freight market
- You return home after a delivery

Deadheading is a normal part of the job, but it's unpaid miles unless you negotiate a deadhead rate with the broker or carrier. Many drivers try to minimize deadhead because it eats into profit.

## Key Differences: Bobtail vs Deadhead

Here's a quick comparison to keep straight:

| Aspect | Bobtail | Deadhead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trailer | None | Empty trailer attached |
| Handling | Lighter rear, more front weight, higher rollover risk | More stable than bobtail, but still lighter than loaded |
| Braking | Rear brakes can lock up easily | Braking distance longer than bobtail, shorter than loaded |
| Fuel economy | Better than loaded, but worse than deadhead due to higher RPMs | Best fuel economy of the three (loaded, bobtail, deadhead) |
| Common use | Pickup, maintenance, terminal moves | Repositioning to next load |
| Pay | Usually unpaid, part of your day | Often unpaid, but sometimes negotiated |
| Risk | Higher risk of jackknife or loss of control | Lower risk than bobtail, but still need care |

## Cost of Bobtailing vs Deadheading

Both cost you money, but deadheading can be more expensive because you're burning fuel and putting miles on the truck without earning revenue. Here are typical costs in 2026:

- **Fuel**: Diesel averages $3.50 to $4.50 per gallon. A bobtail truck gets about 8 to 10 mpg, while a deadhead combination gets 6 to 8 mpg. So 100 miles of bobtail costs $35 to $56 in fuel, and 100 miles of deadhead costs $44 to $75.
- **Maintenance**: More miles mean more wear on tires, brakes, and engine. Budget $0.15 to $0.25 per mile for maintenance, whether bobtail or deadhead.
- **Time**: Your time is worth $25 to $40 per hour as an owner-operator. If you spend 2 hours deadheading, that's $50 to $80 of lost revenue.
- **Insurance**: Bobtail insurance is often cheaper than full coverage, but if you're deadheading under a carrier's authority, you're covered by their liability. Owner-operators need bobtail insurance if they run under their own authority, costing $50 to $150 per month.

## Safety Considerations for Bobtailing

Bobtailing is more dangerous than many drivers think. Without a trailer, the rear axle has less weight, so the drive wheels can lose traction easily. Here's how to stay safe:

- **Brake early and gently**: The rear brakes can lock up, causing a skid. Apply brakes smoothly and leave extra following distance.
- **Watch your turns**: The tractor turns sharper without a trailer, but the rear can swing wide. Check your mirrors constantly.
- **Avoid hard acceleration**: Spinning the drive wheels is easy, especially on grades or wet roads. Use lower gears and steady throttle.
- **Be careful in curves**: The center of gravity is higher, so there's a higher rollover risk. Slow down before curves, not in them.

## Safety Considerations for Deadheading

Deadheading feels more stable than bobtailing, but an empty trailer can still be tricky:

- **Empty trailers bounce**: The suspension is stiff, so the trailer can bounce over bumps, reducing traction. Slow down on rough roads.
- **Braking distance is longer than bobtail**: You have more weight, so give yourself more room to stop.
- **Crosswinds affect empty trailers**: A light trailer can be pushed around. Grip the wheel firmly and reduce speed in high winds.
- **Backing is different**: Without cargo, the trailer is lighter and can jackknife more easily. Go slow and use your spotter.

## How to Minimize Deadhead Miles

Deadhead miles are pure cost, so smart planning helps:

- **Use load boards strategically**: Look for loads that start near your drop-off location. Apps like DAT and Truckstop show live load availability.
- **Negotiate deadhead pay**: If you're an owner-operator, ask the broker for a deadhead rate, often $1.50 to $2.50 per mile for the empty miles.
- **Plan your route**: Avoid empty backhauls by finding a return load before you leave. Some carriers offer incentives for covering deadhead.
- **Consider a relay**: If you're with a fleet, see if you can swap trailers with another driver to reduce empty miles.

## Insurance and Compliance: Bobtail vs Deadhead

Insurance requirements differ:

- **Bobtail insurance**: Covers you when you're driving without a trailer, but not when you're under a load. Owner-operators need this if they run under their own authority. Cost: $50 to $150 per month.
- **Deadhead coverage**: If you're leased to a carrier, their liability insurance covers you when you're pulling their trailer, even empty. If you're on your own authority, your cargo and liability policies cover deadhead, but check your policy.

For compliance, both bobtail and deadhead count as driving time under Hours of Service (HOS). You must log all driving, whether loaded or empty. There's no exemption for bobtailing or deadheading.

## FAQ

**Is bobtailing harder on the truck than deadheading?**
Yes, bobtailing can be harder on the drivetrain because the rear axle is lighter, causing more wheel spin and stress on the differential. Deadheading is gentler on the truck but still adds wear.

**Can I get paid for deadhead miles?**
Sometimes. If you're an owner-operator, you can negotiate a deadhead rate with the broker. Many carriers pay a reduced rate for empty miles, but it's not guaranteed.

**Do I need bobtail insurance if I'm leased to a carrier?**
Usually not, because the carrier's liability covers you when you're bobtailing under their authority. But if you run under your own authority, you need bobtail insurance.

**Does bobtailing use more fuel than deadheading?**
Bobtailing uses less fuel per mile than deadheading because the truck is lighter. But deadheading has better fuel economy than a loaded truck. The cost per mile is lower for bobtail, but you're still burning fuel without earning.

## The Bottom Line

Bobtailing and deadheading are both necessary parts of trucking, but they're not the same. Bobtailing is riskier to drive, while deadheading costs more in fuel and time. Know which one you're doing, log your hours correctly, and plan your routes to minimize empty miles. If you're an owner-operator, negotiate deadhead pay when you can, and always carry the right insurance. Stay safe out there, and keep the shiny side up.
