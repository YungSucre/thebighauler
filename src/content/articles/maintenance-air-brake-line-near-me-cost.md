If you're asking "air brake line near me cost," the short answer is: expect to pay $150 to $600 for a single line replacement at a shop, depending on the line type, length, and labor rates. Parts alone run $20 to $120, and labor typically adds $100 to $400. But the final number depends on whether you go to a dealership, an independent shop, or do it yourself. This guide breaks down the costs, what affects them, and how to get a fair price.

## What Affects the Cost of an Air Brake Line

Several factors drive the price up or down. Knowing them helps you avoid overpaying and ensures you get the right repair.

**Line type and material**
- Nylon (PA12) coil or straight lines: $20 to $60 per line. These are the most common on modern trucks.
- Rubber hose with fittings: $30 to $80 per line. Often used on older rigs or for flexible connections.
- Stainless steel braided: $50 to $120 per line. More durable but rarely needed for standard air systems.

**Length and routing**
A short line between the gladhand and the brake chamber might be 2 to 4 feet. A line running from the front of the trailer to the rear axle can be 20 to 40 feet. Longer lines cost more in materials and labor because they take more time to route and secure.

**Labor rates**
- Independent truck repair shop: $80 to $120 per hour.
- Dealership service center: $120 to $180 per hour.
- Mobile mechanic: $100 to $150 per hour plus a trip charge of $50 to $150.

**Location and urgency**
Urban areas and roadside emergency calls cost more. If you're stuck on the shoulder, expect a premium of 20% to 50% over shop rates.

## How to Get an Accurate Quote Near You

Don't just call one shop. Follow these steps to get a fair price and avoid surprises.

1. **Identify the exact line you need.** Look at the routing, measure the length, and note the fitting types (e.g., 1/4" or 3/8" compression, push-to-connect, or gladhand). Take a photo with your phone.
2. **Call at least three shops.** Ask for a quote over the phone. Give them the make, model, and year of your truck, plus the line description. If they can't quote without seeing it, ask for a ballpark range.
3. **Ask about parts markup.** Some shops charge 30% to 50% over retail for parts. Ask if you can supply your own parts to save money. Many shops allow it, but they may not warranty the labor if the part fails.
4. **Check online reviews.** Look for shops that specialize in commercial trucks. A shop that works on cars might not have the right fittings or experience.
5. **Get it in writing.** Before authorizing work, ask for a written estimate that includes parts, labor, and any shop fees. This protects you from surprise charges.

## DIY vs. Professional Replacement: What's Worth It?

If you're handy and have basic tools, replacing an air brake line is a manageable job. Here's what you need to know.

**Tools and materials**
- Replacement line (pre-made or bulk roll)
- Tubing cutter
- Reamer or deburring tool
- Wrenches (usually 7/16" to 3/4")
- Thread sealant or Teflon tape (if using threaded fittings)
- Safety glasses and gloves

**Steps for a basic line replacement**
1. Park on level ground, chock the wheels, and drain the air tanks to zero pressure.
2. Remove the old line by loosening the fittings. Note how it's routed and secured.
3. Cut the new line to length using a tubing cutter. Deburr the ends.
4. Install the fittings (if not pre-made) and tighten to spec, typically 15 to 25 ft-lbs for compression fittings.
5. Route the new line along the same path, using zip ties or clamps to secure it away from heat sources and moving parts.
6. Pressurize the system and check for leaks with soapy water.

**When to call a pro**
- If the line is in a tight spot or requires removing other parts.
- If you don't have the right tools or experience.
- If you're not 100% sure about the routing or fitting type. A mistake can cause brake failure.

DIY can save you $100 to $300 in labor, but only if you do it right. If you're not confident, pay the shop.

## How to Save Money on Air Brake Line Replacement

Here are practical ways to cut costs without cutting corners.

- **Buy parts online.** Sites like TruckPro, FleetPride, or Amazon sell pre-made lines for $20 to $50. Compare with local shop prices.
- **Use a mobile mechanic for small jobs.** If it's a single line and you're at a truck stop, a mobile mechanic might be cheaper than towing to a shop.
- **Bundle repairs.** If you need other maintenance (e.g., brake pads, oil change), have it done at the same time to save on labor.
- **Check warranty.** If your truck is under warranty, the repair might be covered. Ask the dealer.
- **Prevent future damage.** Use line guards or loom to protect lines from chafing and road debris. This can save you from repeat replacements.

## FAQ

**How long does an air brake line last?**
Typically 5 to 10 years, depending on exposure to heat, UV, and road salt. Inspect lines annually for cracks, bulges, or chafing.

**Can I use a rubber hose instead of nylon?**
Yes, but make sure it's rated for air brake service (SAE J1402 or similar). Rubber may be more flexible but can degrade faster.

**Do I need to replace the fittings too?**
Often yes, especially if they're corroded or damaged. Reusing old fittings can cause leaks. Budget an extra $5 to $15 per fitting.

**Is it safe to drive with a small air leak?**
No. Even a small leak can cause brake imbalance and increase stopping distance. Fix it immediately.
