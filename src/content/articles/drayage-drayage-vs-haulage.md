If you're new to trucking, you've likely heard the terms drayage and haulage used interchangeably. They are not the same. Drayage is a specific type of haulage focused on short-distance moves, usually to or from a port or rail yard. Haulage is the broader term for moving freight by truck over any distance. This guide breaks down the differences in operations, costs, and regulations so you can decide which fits your business model.

## What Is Drayage?

Drayage refers to the trucking of goods over short distances, typically less than 50 miles. The most common drayage jobs are moving shipping containers between ports, rail ramps, and nearby warehouses or distribution centers. You'll also see drayage used for transloading, where cargo is transferred from one container to another, or from a container to a trailer.

Key characteristics of drayage:
- Short hauls, often under 30 miles one way
- Frequent stops and starts, often in congested port areas
- Requires a chassis to carry the container
- Often involves waiting time at the port or rail yard
- May require a TWIC card for port access

## What Is Haulage?

Haulage is the general term for transporting goods by road. It covers everything from local deliveries to long-haul cross-country runs. Haulage can be full truckload (FTL), less-than-truckload (LTL), or specialized freight like refrigerated or flatbed. The distance can be 100 miles or 2,000 miles.

Key characteristics of haulage:
- Variable distances, from local to long-haul
- Uses standard dry van, reefer, flatbed, or specialized trailers
- Less waiting time compared to drayage
- More predictable schedules, especially for regular routes
- Regulations focus on hours of service (HOS) and ELD compliance

## Key Differences: Drayage vs Haulage

The main differences come down to distance, equipment, and the nature of the work. Here's a side-by-side comparison:

| Aspect | Drayage | Haulage |
|--------|---------|---------|
| Distance | Typically under 50 miles | Can be 50 to 2,000+ miles |
| Equipment | Container chassis, often with twist locks | Dry van, reefer, flatbed, etc. |
| Loading/Unloading | Often at ports, rail ramps, or transload facilities | At warehouses, distribution centers, or customer docks |
| Wait times | High, due to port congestion and paperwork | Lower, but can vary |
| Documentation | Port paperwork, customs documents, container seals | Bill of lading, delivery receipts |
| Regulatory focus | TWIC, port security, chassis inspections | HOS, ELD, DOT inspections |
| Pay structure | Often per move or per container | Per mile or per load |
| Entry barriers | Requires TWIC, port appointments, chassis knowledge | Requires CDL, DOT compliance, and equipment |

## Cost Comparison

Costs vary widely, but here are realistic 2026 figures to help you plan:

- **Drayage rates**: $75 to $150 per move, plus fuel surcharges. Some ports pay per container, with rates ranging from $100 to $300 depending on distance and congestion.
- **Haulage rates**: $1.80 to $2.50 per mile for dry van, with reefer and flatbed commanding $2.50 to $3.50 per mile. Local haulage may be hourly, $50 to $80 per hour.
- **Equipment costs**: A used chassis for drayage runs $8,000 to $15,000. A used dry van trailer runs $15,000 to $30,000. Leasing a chassis is $150 to $300 per month.
- **Insurance**: Drayage insurance is often higher due to port risks, averaging $8,000 to $12,000 per year for a single truck. Haulage insurance averages $6,000 to $10,000 per year.

## Which One Should You Choose?

Your choice depends on your goals, location, and equipment.

**Choose drayage if:**
- You live near a major port or rail hub (e.g., Los Angeles, Long Beach, Savannah, New York/New Jersey)
- You prefer shorter days and being home every night
- You're willing to deal with port congestion and waiting
- You can invest in a TWIC card and chassis

**Choose haulage if:**
- You want more variety in routes and freight
- You prefer longer hauls with more predictable schedules
- You're okay with being away from home for days or weeks
- You want to use standard trailers that are easier to resell

## Practical Steps to Get Started

If you're leaning toward drayage, here's what to do this week:
1. **Get your TWIC card**: Apply at a TSA enrollment center. It takes about 2-4 weeks and costs $125.25 for five years.
2. **Research port requirements**: Visit the port's website for drayage trucker requirements. Some ports require additional background checks or appointments.
3. **Find a chassis provider**: Look into leasing vs. owning. Many drayage companies provide chassis, but if you're independent, you'll need one.
4. **Get port insurance quotes**: Call three insurance brokers who specialize in drayage. Compare rates and coverage.

If you're leaning toward haulage:
1. **Decide on freight type**: Dry van is the easiest entry, but reefer and flatbed pay more.
2. **Get your CDL**: If you don't have it, enroll in a CDL school. Costs range from $3,000 to $10,000.
3. **Find a carrier to lease on with**: Many companies offer lease-purchase programs, but be cautious of high payments.
4. **Set up your ELD**: You'll need an ELD compliant with FMCSA rules. Costs range from $20 to $50 per month for a basic device.

## FAQ

**Can I do both drayage and haulage?**
Yes, many owner-operators mix both. For example, you might do a drayage move in the morning and a local haul in the afternoon. Just be aware of HOS limits and port appointment times.

**Do I need a special license for drayage?**
No, a standard Class A CDL is sufficient. But you'll need a TWIC card for port access, and some ports require a background check.

**Is drayage more profitable than haulage?**
It depends. Drayage can be profitable if you minimize wait times and have steady volume. Haulage offers more miles, but also more fuel and maintenance costs. Many drivers find drayage pays better per hour, while haulage pays better per mile.

**What are the biggest challenges in drayage?**
Port congestion, appointment delays, and chassis availability are the top issues. You need to be patient and good at managing your time.

## The Bottom Line

Drayage and haulage are both viable trucking careers, but they require different skills and equipment. Drayage is short-haul, port-focused, and often involves more waiting, but it allows you to be home more. Haulage offers more variety and miles, but longer time away. Assess your location, budget, and lifestyle preferences. If you're near a port, drayage can be a steady niche. If you prefer the open road, haulage is your path. Either way, start with the practical steps above to get moving.
