If you're trying to decide between hauling spot loads or locking in contract rates, the short answer is: contract rates give you predictable income, while spot quotes can pay more per mile but come with feast-or-famine swings. Most owner-operators run a mix, but the right balance depends on your risk tolerance, cash flow, and how well you know your lanes. Here's what you need to know to make the call.

## What Are Spot Freight Quotes?

Spot freight is a single load posted on a load board or broker platform, priced at the current market rate. You take it as a one-off, no ongoing commitment. Spot rates move daily based on supply and demand, fuel prices, weather, and season.

In early 2026, spot rates for dry van average $1.80 to $2.20 per mile, but can spike to $3.00 or more during peak seasons like Q4 retail. Reefers run $2.20 to $2.60, flatbeds $2.00 to $2.40. These are national averages; your actual rate depends on lane, equipment, and urgency.

### Pros of Spot Freight
- Higher potential pay per mile, especially on hot lanes or tight deadlines.
- Flexibility to choose loads that fit your schedule and home time.
- No long-term commitment if a broker or shipper turns out to be problematic.

### Cons of Spot Freight
- Income is unpredictable. You might have great weeks and dead weeks.
- More time spent on load boards, negotiating, and vetting brokers.
- No guarantee of backhaul, so you may run empty miles.

## What Are Contract Rates?

Contract rates are agreed-upon prices for moving freight for a specific shipper over a set period, usually 6 to 12 months. You sign a contract that locks in a rate per mile or per load, often with minimum volume commitments.

In 2026, contract rates for dry van average $2.00 to $2.40 per mile, slightly higher than spot on average, but the spread narrows when spot spikes. Contract rates include fuel surcharges, accessorials, and sometimes detention pay, but they're fixed, so you know what to expect.

### Pros of Contract Rates
- Steady, predictable revenue. You can plan your budget, maintenance, and payments.
- Less time hunting for loads. The shipper or broker assigns you freight.
- Better relationships with shippers, which can lead to priority treatment and consistent volume.

### Cons of Contract Rates
- Lower per-mile pay when spot rates spike. You're locked in.
- Less flexibility. You must be available for scheduled pickups and deliveries.
- Potential for deadhead if the contract doesn't cover repositioning.

## How to Decide: 5 Practical Steps

1. **Track your actual cost per mile.** Use your TMS or a spreadsheet to calculate your all-in cost per mile, including fuel, maintenance, insurance, and payments. In 2026, that's typically $1.60 to $2.00 for a dry van owner-operator. If spot rates dip below your cost, you're losing money.

2. **Assess your cash reserve.** If you have 3 to 6 months of expenses saved, you can ride out spot market dips. If not, contract rates provide a safety net.

3. **Test the waters with a small contract.** Start with one or two contract lanes that you know well, and keep the rest of your loads spot. This gives you a feel for the commitment without going all-in.

4. **Negotiate contract rates with a floor.** Some contracts allow for rate adjustments if fuel prices spike or if the lane changes. Ask for a minimum rate per mile and a fuel surcharge formula.

5. **Review your mix quarterly.** The market changes. In a tight market, spot may pay better; in a soft market, contracts win. Rebalance based on current data.

## Real-World Scenarios

**Scenario A: New owner-operator with a truck payment.** You need consistent income to cover $3,000 to $5,000 in monthly fixed costs. A contract with a reliable shipper at $2.10/mile over 8,000 miles a month gives you $16,800 gross, which covers your costs and leaves a margin. Spot could pay $2.50/mile, but if you only find 5,000 miles, you're at $12,500, which might not cut it.

**Scenario B: Experienced driver with no debt.** You can afford to play the spot market. You target high-paying lanes, take time off when rates drop, and average $2.30/mile over the year, beating contract rates. But you also deal with weeks where you sit for two days waiting for a load.

**Scenario C: Fleet owner with 5 trucks.** You need to keep trucks moving to cover driver pay. A mix of 70% contract and 30% spot gives you baseline revenue and lets you capture upside when spot spikes. This is the most common approach among small fleets.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I mix spot and contract loads?**
Yes. Many carriers run a blended strategy. For example, you might have a contract with one shipper for 3 loads a week, and fill the rest with spot loads. This balances stability and upside.

**Q: How do I find contract rates?**
Reach out to shippers directly or work with a freight broker who can match you with contract opportunities. Load boards like DAT and Truckstop also list contract lanes. You can also join a freight network or use a TMS that offers rate benchmarking.

**Q: What's the typical contract length?**
Most contracts run 6 to 12 months, with options to renew. Some are longer for dedicated lanes. Make sure you understand the termination clause and volume commitments before signing.

**Q: Are contract rates always lower than spot?**
Not necessarily. In a soft market, contract rates can be higher than spot because they provide guaranteed volume. In a hot market, spot outpaces contracts. The key is to compare current rates in your lanes.

## The Bottom Line

Spot freight and contract rates serve different purposes. Spot gives you flexibility and upside, but it's risky. Contracts give you stability and peace of mind, but you might leave money on the table during peaks. The best approach is to know your numbers, keep a cash cushion, and start with a small contract if you're new. Reassess every quarter. In 2026, the market is still recovering, so a balanced mix of 50% spot and 50% contract is a safe starting point for most owner-operators. Test, track, and adjust based on your actual experience.
