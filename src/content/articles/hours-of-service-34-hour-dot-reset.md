The 34 hour reset is a provision in the Hours of Service (HOS) rules that lets you restart your 60 or 70 hour driving window by taking at least 34 consecutive hours off duty. It is one of the most powerful tools you have to maximize your weekly driving time, but only if you use it correctly. This guide explains the rule, how to apply it, and how to plan your week around it.

## What Is the 34 Hour Reset?

The 34 hour reset is a period of at least 34 consecutive hours where you are not driving, and you are not on duty. After this break, your 60 hour (for property carrying) or 70 hour (for passenger carrying or if your carrier uses the 70 hour rule) clock resets to zero. This means you can start a new week with a full 60 or 70 hours of driving time.

Key points:
- The reset must be at least 34 hours, but it can be longer if you want.
- You must be completely off duty, meaning no work, no driving, no loading, no waiting for loading. You can be in the sleeper berth, at home, or at a hotel.
- You cannot use the reset to extend a single day's driving; it only resets the weekly limit.

## How the 60 and 70 Hour Limits Work

Before you plan a reset, you need to know which weekly limit applies to you.

- **60 hour rule:** If your carrier does not operate every day of the week, you are limited to 60 hours of driving in 7 consecutive days. After 60 hours, you must take a 34 hour reset before driving again.
- **70 hour rule:** If your carrier operates every day of the week, you are limited to 70 hours of driving in 8 consecutive days. After 70 hours, you must take a 34 hour reset.

Most long haul drivers are on the 70 hour rule. Your carrier's compliance department will tell you which one you are under.

## When Can You Take a 34 Hour Reset?

You can take a reset at any time, but it only makes sense when you are close to your weekly limit. For example, if you have driven 65 hours in 8 days, you have 5 hours left before you hit 70. You could drive those 5 hours, then take a reset. Or you could take the reset early, but that would waste driving time.

A common strategy is to run your clock down to near zero, then take a 34 hour reset. This gives you a full 70 hours for the next week. However, you must also respect the 14 hour on duty limit and the 11 hour driving limit each day. The reset does not change those daily rules.

## How to Plan Your Week Around the Reset

Here is a practical step by step approach to using the 34 hour reset effectively.

1. **Know your weekly limit.** Check your ELD or logbook to see how many hours you have used in the current 7 or 8 day period.
2. **Decide when to stop.** When you are within 1 to 2 hours of your limit, find a safe place to park. Do not push to the exact limit if you are not sure you can find parking.
3. **Start the reset.** Log off duty. Make sure you are in a location where you can legally park for 34 hours, such as a truck stop, rest area, or shipper/receiver lot with permission.
4. **Track the time.** Set a timer for 34 hours. Remember that you must have 34 consecutive hours off duty. If you have to move the truck for any reason, the clock resets and you must start over.
5. **Plan your next week.** Once the reset is complete, you have a fresh 60 or 70 hours. Plan your loads accordingly.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Not taking a full 34 hours.** If you take 33 hours and 59 minutes, the reset does not count. You must have the full 34.
- **Doing any work during the reset.** Even answering a work call or checking a load board might count as on duty if you are not careful. Stay completely off duty.
- **Using the reset to extend a day.** The reset does not give you more than 11 hours of driving in a day. It only resets the weekly clock.
- **Forgetting the 14 hour rule.** After the reset, you still have to comply with the 14 hour on duty limit. You cannot drive after 14 hours of on duty time.

## Realistic Costs and Benefits

Taking a 34 hour reset means you are not driving for at least 34 hours. For an owner operator, that is lost revenue. However, it is mandatory if you want to keep driving legally. The cost of not taking a reset can be much higher: fines, out of service orders, and potential loss of your CDL.

- **Fine for HOS violation:** $1,000 to $16,000 per violation, depending on severity.
- **Out of service:** You can be placed out of service for 24 hours if you violate HOS rules.
- **Lost revenue during reset:** If you average $1.50 per mile and drive 500 miles per day, a 34 hour reset costs you about $750 in gross revenue. But that is the price of compliance.

Some drivers use the reset to do maintenance, laundry, or rest. That can offset the cost by improving your health and safety.

## FAQ

### Can I split the 34 hour reset into two breaks?

No. The 34 hours must be consecutive. You cannot take 20 hours off, drive for 2 hours, then take another 14 hours off and call it a reset.

### Does the 34 hour reset apply to the 14 hour rule?

No. The 14 hour rule is a daily limit. The reset only resets the weekly 60 or 70 hour limit. You still must stop driving after 14 hours of on duty time each day.

### Can I use the sleeper berth for the reset?

Yes. You can spend the entire 34 hours in the sleeper berth, as long as you are off duty. You cannot be in the sleeper berth and also be on duty for any part of the reset.

### What if I am on the 60 hour rule and I take a reset?

If you are on the 60 hour rule, the reset resets your 60 hour clock. You can then drive up to 60 hours in the next 7 days.

## The Bottom Line

The 34 hour reset is a simple but essential rule. It allows you to reset your weekly driving clock, but only if you take a full 34 consecutive hours off duty. Plan your week around it, track your hours carefully, and never cut the reset short. The cost of a reset is real, but the cost of a violation is far worse. Use the reset to rest, maintain your truck, and come back refreshed and legal.

Always check the FMCSA website for the latest HOS rules, as they can change. This guide reflects the rules as of June 2026.
