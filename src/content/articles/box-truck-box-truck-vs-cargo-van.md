Choosing between a box truck and a cargo van depends on what you haul, how far you go, and what you can afford. Box trucks offer more space and payload, but they cost more to buy, fuel, and insure. Cargo vans are cheaper to run and easier to drive, but they limit you to lighter, smaller loads. Here is a straight comparison to help you decide.

## Key Differences at a Glance

| Feature | Box Truck | Cargo Van |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Typical GVWR | 10,000 to 26,000 lbs | 8,500 to 11,000 lbs |
| Payload Capacity | 4,000 to 12,000 lbs | 2,000 to 3,500 lbs |
| Cargo Volume | 400 to 1,200 cu ft | 200 to 400 cu ft |
| Fuel Economy | 8 to 12 mpg | 14 to 20 mpg |
| Purchase Price (New) | $40,000 to $80,000 | $30,000 to $50,000 |
| CDL Required | Often over 26,000 lbs | No |
| Parking | Needs more space | Fits standard spots |

## Cost of Ownership

**Upfront Costs**

A new cargo van like a Ford Transit or Mercedes Sprinter runs $35,000 to $55,000. A new box truck, such as a Freightliner M2 or Isuzu NPR, starts around $45,000 and can hit $80,000 for a larger model. Used options vary widely, but expect $15,000 to $35,000 for a cargo van and $25,000 to $50,000 for a box truck with 100,000 to 200,000 miles.

**Operating Costs**

Fuel is a major factor. At $3.50 per gallon, a cargo van at 16 mpg costs about $0.22 per mile. A box truck at 10 mpg costs $0.35 per mile. Over 20,000 miles a year, that is a $2,600 difference. Maintenance is higher for box trucks due to diesel engines and heavier components. Insurance also runs higher: expect $150 to $300 per month for a cargo van, and $300 to $600 for a box truck, depending on your record and coverage.

## Licensing and Regulations

If your box truck has a GVWR over 26,000 lbs, you need a CDL. Most box trucks under that weight, like a 26-foot unit, can be driven with a standard license. Cargo vans almost always stay under the CDL threshold. However, if you cross state lines, you may need a DOT number and to follow hours of service rules. Check the FMCSA website for current requirements as of 2026.

## Which One Fits Your Business?

**Cargo Van: Best for**

- Local deliveries of packages, food, or small furniture
- Moving household goods for small moves
- Running a courier or last-mile service
- Low startup costs and easy parking

**Box Truck: Best for**

- Moving large furniture, appliances, or palletized freight
- Multi-stop routes with heavy loads
- Freight hauling on load boards like DAT or Truckstop
- Building a business that can scale to heavier loads

## Practical Steps to Decide This Week

1. **List your typical loads.** Weigh a few samples. If they exceed 3,500 lbs, a cargo van won't work.
2. **Measure your cargo volume.** Use a tape measure on your largest items. Compare to van and box truck dimensions.
3. **Calculate your per-mile cost.** Use the fuel figures above and your expected monthly mileage.
4. **Check your local parking.** If you can't park a 26-foot truck at home or in a lot, a van may be simpler.
5. **Talk to an insurance agent.** Get quotes for both types with your driving record.

## FAQ

**Can I drive a box truck without a CDL?**
Yes, as long as the GVWR is 26,000 lbs or less. Many 16 to 20 foot box trucks fall under this limit. Verify the exact GVWR on the door sticker.

**Which is better for a first-time owner-operator?**
A cargo van is easier to start with because of lower costs and no CDL requirement. You can build experience and save for a box truck later.

**How much can a cargo van really haul?**
Most cargo vans carry 2,000 to 3,500 lbs. Check the payload rating on the door. Overloading can damage the van and void warranties.

**Do box trucks need a DOT number?**
If you operate interstate and the vehicle is over 10,001 lbs, you likely need a DOT number and to follow federal regulations. Intrastate rules vary by state, so check with your state's DOT.

## The Bottom Line

Choose a cargo van if you want lower costs, easier driving, and you handle light loads. Choose a box truck if you need more space and payload, and you can handle higher expenses. Both can make money, but the right choice depends on your specific freight and budget. Start with a clear list of your loads and costs, then test drive both to see what fits your operation.
