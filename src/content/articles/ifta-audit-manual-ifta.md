An IFTA audit can be stressful, but if you keep accurate records and know what to expect, you can get through it without penalties. This guide explains how to handle an IFTA audit manually, without expensive software, using practical steps that work for owner-operators and small fleets.

## What Triggers an IFTA Audit?

IFTA audits are not random. States select carriers based on red flags in their quarterly tax returns. Common triggers include:

- **Inconsistent fuel mileage**: Your average miles per gallon (MPG) falls outside the normal range of 4 to 8 MPG for heavy trucks.
- **Zero or very low miles in a jurisdiction**: If you travel through a state but report no miles there, auditors notice.
- **Late filings or payments**: Repeated late submissions increase your audit risk.
- **Large refunds or credits**: Claiming excessive fuel tax credits without supporting receipts raises suspicion.
- **Audit history**: If you've been audited before and had issues, you're more likely to be audited again.

## What Records Do You Need?

For a manual IFTA audit, you must produce three main types of records for the audit period (usually the last four quarters, but sometimes up to three years):

1. **Distance records**: Trip sheets, logbooks, GPS reports, or any document showing miles driven in each jurisdiction.
2. **Fuel records**: Receipts, invoices, or credit card statements showing fuel purchased in each jurisdiction.
3. **Vehicle records**: Registration, title, and mileage records for each qualified motor vehicle.

Keep these records for at least four years from the due date of the return, or longer if your state requires it.

## How to Organize Your Manual Records

If you're not using electronic logging devices (ELDs) or fuel card reports, you can still pass an audit with paper records. Here's a simple system:

- **Use a binder or accordion file** with dividers for each quarter.
- **Keep a trip sheet for every trip**: Date, starting and ending odometer readings, routes, and total miles per state.
- **Staple fuel receipts to the corresponding trip sheet** or file them by date.
- **Summarize monthly**: At the end of each month, total miles per state and fuel gallons per state. This makes quarterly reporting easier.
- **Reconcile odometer readings**: Your total miles per trip should match your vehicle's odometer. Auditors will check for gaps.

## Step-by-Step: Preparing for the Audit

When you receive an audit notice, don't panic. Follow these steps:

1. **Read the notice carefully**: It will state the audit period, what records are required, and the deadline.
2. **Gather all records**: Pull trip sheets, fuel receipts, and vehicle documents for the audit period.
3. **Check for missing items**: If you're missing receipts, contact fuel suppliers for duplicates. Some states accept credit card statements if they show gallons and location.
4. **Calculate your MPG**: Divide total miles by total gallons for the period. If it's outside 4 to 8 MPG, be ready to explain (e.g., heavy loads, mountainous terrain).
5. **Review your quarterly returns**: Make sure your reported miles and gallons match your records. If there are errors, correct them before the audit.
6. **Prepare a summary sheet**: List total miles per state, total gallons per state, and MPG for the entire period. This shows the auditor you're organized.

## Common Audit Findings and How to Avoid Them

Auditors often find these issues in manual records:

- **Missing fuel receipts**: Without proof of purchase, you can't claim fuel tax credits. Always keep receipts, even for cash purchases.
- **Inaccurate state mileage**: If you guess miles, you'll be off. Use a paper map or a simple mileage log to track state lines.
- **Odometer discrepancies**: If your trip sheets don't add up to the odometer, auditors will question your records. Keep a log of your odometer at the start and end of each trip.
- **Unreported miles in certain states**: Even if you only drive 10 miles through a corner of a state, you must report it. Use a state line crossing log.

## What Happens During the Audit?

The auditor will review your records, ask questions, and may inspect your vehicles. They will compare your reported miles and gallons to your records. If everything matches, you'll get a clean bill. If not, you may owe additional taxes plus interest and penalties.

Penalties for IFTA violations vary by state, but can range from 10% to 50% of the tax due, plus interest. In severe cases, your IFTA license can be revoked.

## Manual vs. Software: Is Manual Enough?

Manual recordkeeping is legal and can pass an audit if you're disciplined. However, it's time-consuming and error-prone. Many owner-operators use a combination of paper logs and fuel card reports. If you're considering software, costs range from $10 to $50 per month for basic IFTA tracking, which can save you hours each quarter.

## FAQ

**Q: How far back can an IFTA audit go?**
A: Typically, auditors review the last four quarters, but they can go back up to three years if they find significant errors.

**Q: What if I lost my fuel receipts?**
A: Contact the fuel station or your fuel card company for duplicates. If you can't get them, you may lose the tax credit for those gallons.

**Q: Can I use my ELD data for IFTA?**
A: Yes, ELD data showing state mileage is acceptable, but you still need fuel receipts. Many ELD systems generate IFTA reports.

**Q: What happens if I owe money after the audit?**
A: You'll receive a notice with the amount due, including interest and penalties. You can usually pay in installments, but interest accrues.

## The Bottom Line

A manual IFTA audit is manageable if you keep organized records and follow the rules. Start by setting up a simple filing system today, and review your records quarterly to catch errors early. If you're consistent, you'll pass an audit without stress. And if you're struggling, consider investing in software to automate the process, but remember: good records are the foundation, whether paper or digital.
