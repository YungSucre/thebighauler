When you look at your truck's tires, you see a maximum pressure number molded into the sidewall, like 120 PSI. Then you open the driver's door and see a sticker with a different number, maybe 105 PSI. Which one is right? The answer is simple: use the door sticker pressure, not the sidewall maximum. The sidewall number is the absolute limit the tire can handle, not what you should run daily. The door sticker is set by the vehicle manufacturer based on your truck's weight and handling. Here's why it matters and how to get it right.

## What the Sidewall Number Means

The pressure printed on the tire sidewall is the maximum cold inflation pressure the tire is designed to withstand. It is not a recommendation for everyday driving. For example, a common steer tire might show 120 PSI max, but running at that pressure when your axle weight doesn't require it can cause a harsh ride, uneven wear in the center of the tread, and reduced traction. The sidewall number is a legal and engineering limit, not a target.

## What the Door Sticker Means

The door sticker, often called the Tire and Loading Information label, lists the original equipment tire size and the recommended cold inflation pressure for the front and rear axles. This number is determined by the truck manufacturer after testing for load capacity, ride quality, and stability. For a typical Class 8 tractor, the sticker might say 105 PSI for steer tires and 100 PSI for drive tires, depending on the tire load index and the truck's gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR). This is the pressure you should use for normal loaded operation.

## Why the Difference Matters

Using the sidewall max instead of the door sticker can lead to several problems:

- **Center tread wear**: Overinflation causes the tire to ride on the center of the tread, wearing it out faster.
- **Reduced traction**: A hard tire has a smaller contact patch, which can hurt braking and cornering, especially in rain.
- **Harsh ride**: Overinflated tires transmit more road shock to the driver and the suspension.
- **Increased blowout risk**: Running at max pressure all the time leaves no margin for heat buildup, especially in summer.

Conversely, underinflation causes shoulder wear, overheating, and poor fuel economy. The door sticker pressure is the balanced choice.

## How to Set Your Tire Pressure Correctly

Follow these steps to get the right pressure for your truck:

1. **Check the door sticker**: Find the label on the driver's side door jamb. Note the recommended PSI for front and rear tires.
2. **Check tire load**: If you're running a different tire size or load than what the sticker assumes, consult the tire manufacturer's load and inflation tables. For example, a tire with a load index of 152 might require 110 PSI for a 12,000 lb steer axle, but only 100 PSI for 10,000 lb.
3. **Check cold pressure**: Measure tire pressure when the tires are cold, meaning the truck hasn't been driven for at least 3 hours or less than 1 mile. Use a calibrated gauge.
4. **Adjust to sticker or table**: Inflate to the recommended cold pressure. If you're loaded heavier than the sticker's assumption, use the tire manufacturer's table to find the correct PSI for your actual axle weight.
5. **Recheck regularly**: Check pressure at least once a week and before long trips. Use a tire pressure monitoring system (TPMS) if your truck has one.

## When to Deviate from the Door Sticker

There are times when you should not use the door sticker pressure:

- **Heavy loads**: If your axle weight exceeds the sticker's assumed load, you need more pressure. For example, if your steer axle is 12,500 lb and the sticker says 105 PSI for 11,000 lb, you might need 110 PSI per the load table.
- **Different tire sizes**: If you've replaced the original tires with a different load range or size, the sticker is no longer accurate.
- **Special applications**: Off-road or winter driving may require lower pressure for traction, but only if you adjust back for highway speeds.

In these cases, use the tire manufacturer's load and inflation tables, which are available online or from your tire dealer. Always err on the side of the higher pressure if you're unsure, but never exceed the sidewall max.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Using the sidewall max as a target**: This is the most common error. Remember, it's a limit, not a recommendation.
- **Checking pressure after driving**: Hot pressure reads higher than cold. Always check cold.
- **Ignoring the spare**: The spare tire also needs the correct pressure, often higher for space-saving spares.
- **Trusting a faulty gauge**: Check your gauge against a known accurate one periodically.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use the sidewall pressure if my truck is fully loaded?**
A: Only if the sidewall max matches the load table requirement. For most trucks, the door sticker is lower than the sidewall max, but if you're at max GVWR, you might need to go up to the sidewall max. Check the load table first.

**Q: What happens if I run 5 PSI over the door sticker?**
A: A small overpressure is not dangerous, but it can cause faster center wear and a slightly harsher ride. Over time, it reduces tire life.

**Q: How often should I check tire pressure?**
A: At least once a week and before any long trip. Also check after a significant temperature change, as pressure drops about 1-2 PSI for every 10°F drop.

**Q: Does the door sticker apply to all tires on the truck?**
A: It typically lists front and rear pressures separately. If you have duals on the drive axle, use the same pressure for both tires in that position.

## The Bottom Line

The door sticker is your go-to for everyday tire pressure. The sidewall number is just a ceiling. For most drivers, setting pressure to the door sticker and adjusting for load using the manufacturer's table will maximize tire life, fuel economy, and safety. Check your pressures cold, at least weekly, and keep a good gauge in the cab. If you're ever in doubt, the tire manufacturer's load table is the final authority, not the sidewall.

Remember, proper inflation saves you money. Underinflated tires can cut fuel economy by 0.5% to 1% per 10 PSI drop, and overinflation can shorten tire life by thousands of miles. Take five minutes this week to check your pressures against the sticker. Your wallet and your safety will thank you.
