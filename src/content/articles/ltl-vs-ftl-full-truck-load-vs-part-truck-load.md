When you need to move freight, the two main options are Full Truck Load (FTL) and Part Truck Load (PTL), also known as Less Than Truckload (LTL). The choice affects your cost, transit time, and risk of damage. This guide breaks down the differences so you can decide which fits your shipment.

## What is Full Truck Load (FTL)?

FTL means your freight occupies the entire trailer. You pay for the whole truck, whether you use all the space or not. This is ideal for large shipments, typically over 10,000 pounds or more than 10 pallets, but it can also be used for smaller loads if you need dedicated transport.

**Pros:**
- Faster transit: no stops for other customers. Direct linehaul.
- Less handling: your freight is loaded once and unloaded once, reducing damage risk.
- More control: you can choose the route and schedule.

**Cons:**
- Higher cost per shipment, especially for small loads.
- You pay for unused space if your load is not full.

## What is Part Truck Load (PTL) or Less Than Truckload (LTL)?

PTL (or LTL) means your freight shares trailer space with other shippers' cargo. You pay only for the space you use, typically by weight and dimensions. Carriers consolidate multiple shipments into one trailer, often via a hub-and-spoke network.

**Pros:**
- Lower cost for small shipments (under 10,000 lbs or 6 pallets).
- Pay only for what you ship.
- Access to a wide network of carriers.

**Cons:**
- Longer transit times due to multiple stops and terminal handling.
- Higher risk of damage or loss from repeated handling.
- Potential for freight reclassification or additional accessorial charges.

## Key Differences: FTL vs PTL

The table below summarizes the main differences.

| Factor | Full Truck Load (FTL) | Part Truck Load (PTL/LTL) |
|--------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| Cost per shipment | Higher (e.g., $1,500-$3,500 for a 1,000-mile haul) | Lower (e.g., $300-$800 for a small pallet) |
| Cost per pound | Lower (e.g., $0.15-$0.30 per lb) | Higher (e.g., $0.50-$1.00 per lb) |
| Transit time | Faster (direct, no stops) | Slower (multiple stops, terminal delays) |
| Handling | Minimal (load once, unload once) | Multiple handlings at terminals |
| Damage risk | Lower | Higher |
| Ideal shipment size | Over 10,000 lbs or 10+ pallets | Under 10,000 lbs or 6 pallets |
| Flexibility | High (dedicated truck) | Low (carrier schedules) |

## Cost Comparison: Realistic Figures (2026)

Rates vary by lane, weight, and fuel prices. As of early 2026, here are typical ranges:

- **FTL (full truckload):** $1.50 to $2.50 per mile for a 53-foot dry van. A 1,000-mile haul costs $1,500 to $2,500. For a 2,000-mile cross-country move, expect $3,000 to $5,000.
- **PTL (LTL):** Rates are based on weight and class. A single pallet (500 lbs) might cost $150 to $300 for 500 miles. A 5,000-lb shipment (5-6 pallets) could run $600 to $1,200 for the same distance. Heavier shipments (10,000 lbs) might be $1,000 to $2,000, but at that point FTL becomes competitive.

**When to choose FTL:**
- Your shipment is over 10,000 lbs or 10 pallets.
- You need expedited or time-sensitive delivery.
- Your freight is fragile or high-value, requiring minimal handling.
- You want to avoid terminal delays.

**When to choose PTL:**
- Your shipment is under 6 pallets or 5,000 lbs.
- You are cost-sensitive and can accept longer transit.
- You have flexible delivery windows.
- You are shipping multiple smaller orders to different destinations (PTL can be more efficient).

## Practical Steps to Decide This Week

1. **Weigh and measure your freight.** Get exact weight and dimensions. Use a pallet calculator to estimate pallet count.
2. **Get quotes from both FTL and PTL carriers.** Use load boards (e.g., DAT, Truckstop) for FTL spot rates, and get LTL quotes from carriers like FedEx Freight, XPO, or Estes. Compare total cost, including fuel surcharges and accessorials.
3. **Check transit times.** Ask for guaranteed delivery dates. If you need it in 2 days, FTL might be the only option.
4. **Evaluate damage risk.** If your freight is fragile, FTL reduces handling. If it's durable, PTL is fine.
5. **Consider consolidation.** If you have a partial load, see if you can combine with another shipment to fill a truck and split the cost (this is essentially PTL, but you control it).

## FAQ

**Q: Is PTL always cheaper than FTL?**
A: Not always. For shipments over 10,000 lbs, FTL can be cheaper per pound because you avoid LTL accessorial charges and reclassification. Always compare total cost.

**Q: How do LTL freight classes affect price?**
A: LTL rates depend on the National Motor Freight Classification (NMFC) class, which is based on density, stowability, handling, and liability. Higher classes cost more. Get your freight class right to avoid reclassification fees.

**Q: Can I use FTL for a small shipment?**
A: Yes, but it's rarely cost-effective unless you need expedited service or have special requirements (e.g., temperature control, security).

**Q: What are accessorial charges in LTL?**
A: These are extra fees for services like liftgate delivery, inside delivery, residential pickup, or detention. They can add $50 to $200 per occurrence. Ask for a full quote.

## The Bottom Line

Choose FTL when you have a full truckload (over 10,000 lbs or 10 pallets), need speed, or want to minimize handling. Choose PTL when your shipment is small, you're cost-conscious, and you can tolerate longer transit. Always get quotes for both options, and factor in all fees. The right choice saves money and avoids headaches.
