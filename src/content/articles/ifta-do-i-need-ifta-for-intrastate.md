If you only run intrastate (within one state), you generally do not need IFTA. IFTA applies to interstate carriers operating in two or more member jurisdictions. But there are exceptions: some states require IFTA for intrastate fuel tax reporting, and certain intrastate operations may still need fuel permits. Here's what you need to know to stay compliant.

## What IFTA Covers and Why It Exists

The International Fuel Tax Agreement (IFTA) is a compact among the 48 contiguous U.S. states and Canadian provinces. It simplifies fuel tax reporting for carriers that travel across multiple jurisdictions. Instead of filing separate fuel tax reports with each state, you file one quarterly IFTA return with your base state, which then distributes taxes to other jurisdictions based on miles driven and fuel purchased.

IFTA applies to motor vehicles used, designed, or maintained for transportation of persons or property that:
- Have two axles and a gross vehicle weight or registered gross weight exceeding 26,000 pounds, or
- Have three or more axles, regardless of weight, or
- Are used in combination with a gross combination weight exceeding 26,000 pounds.

If you operate only within one state, you don't cross state lines, so IFTA's interstate purpose doesn't apply. However, states have their own fuel tax rules, and some require intrastate carriers to hold an IFTA license or a similar fuel permit.

## When Intrastate Might Still Require IFTA

Even if you never leave your home state, you may need IFTA in these situations:

- **Your state requires IFTA for intrastate carriers.** A few states, like Kentucky and New York, mandate IFTA for certain intrastate vehicles, usually those over 26,000 pounds. Check with your state's Department of Revenue or Motor Carrier Division.
- **You travel into another state even occasionally.** If you cross a state line for any reason, even for a few miles, you're now interstate and must register for IFTA.
- **Your vehicle is registered in a state that participates in IFTA.** Even if you only run intrastate, your base state may require you to have an IFTA license to track fuel use, especially if you have a heavy vehicle.
- **You operate in a state with a separate fuel tax permit.** Some states, like Oregon, have their own fuel tax agreements (Oregon uses a weight-mile tax, not IFTA). You may need a state fuel permit instead.

## State-by-State Examples (2026)

Here's a snapshot of how some states treat intrastate carriers:

| State | Intrastate IFTA Required? | Notes |
|-------|---------------------------|-------|
| Texas | No | Intrastate carriers do not need IFTA; they pay Texas fuel taxes on fuel purchased in state. |
| California | No | Intrastate carriers are exempt from IFTA, but must pay California fuel tax. |
| Kentucky | Yes | Intrastate vehicles over 26,000 lbs must have IFTA and file quarterly returns. |
| New York | Yes | Intrastate carriers with heavy vehicles must register for IFTA, even if they don't cross state lines. |
| Florida | No | Intrastate carriers are not required to have IFTA, but must have a Florida fuel tax decal. |
| Oregon | No | Oregon uses a weight-mile tax; intrastate carriers need an Oregon fuel tax permit, not IFTA. |

Always verify with your state's motor carrier division, as rules can change.

## How to Determine Your Requirement

Follow these steps to know if you need IFTA:

1. **Check your operation type.** Do you cross any state line, even for a short trip? If yes, you're interstate and need IFTA.
2. **Check your vehicle weight.** If your GVW or GCW is over 26,000 pounds, you're likely subject to IFTA if interstate, and possibly intrastate in some states.
3. **Contact your state's Department of Revenue or Motor Carrier Division.** Ask directly: "Do intrastate carriers need an IFTA license?" Get it in writing if possible.
4. **Review your state's fuel tax laws.** Some states have separate fuel permits for intrastate carriers, like a fuel decal or license.
5. **Consult a fuel tax specialist or your TMS software provider.** Many ELD and dispatch systems include IFTA tracking; they can help you determine requirements.

## What Happens If You Skip IFTA When Required?

If you're required to have IFTA and don't, you risk:
- Fines and penalties from states where you operate (often $100 to $500 per violation).
- Back taxes owed, plus interest.
- Being placed out of service during a DOT inspection.
- Losing your ability to operate in certain states.

Even if you think you don't need IFTA, it's safer to confirm with the state than to assume.

## Practical Steps for This Week

- **Call your state's motor carrier office.** Use the number on their website. Ask the specific question about intrastate IFTA requirements.
- **Review your routes.** If you ever cross a state line, even for a short turn, you need IFTA.
- **Check your vehicle registration.** If your truck is registered over 26,000 pounds, note that some states link registration to IFTA.
- **Set up a fuel tracking system.** Even if you don't need IFTA, tracking fuel purchases and miles is good practice for tax deductions and fuel management.

## FAQ

**Can I operate intrastate without IFTA if my truck is over 26,000 pounds?**

Yes, in most states, as long as you never cross state lines. But some states like Kentucky and New York require IFTA for intrastate heavy vehicles. Always check with your state.

**What if I occasionally cross into another state for a few miles?**

Then you are interstate and must register for IFTA. There's no minimum distance threshold. Even a short trip across a state line triggers IFTA.

**Do I need IFTA for a straight truck under 26,000 pounds?**

Probably not, because IFTA only applies to vehicles over 26,000 pounds. But some states have lower thresholds for fuel permits, so verify.

**Is IFTA the same as a fuel permit?**

No. IFTA is an interstate fuel tax agreement. Some states have separate fuel permits for intrastate carriers, like a fuel decal or license. You may need one even if you don't need IFTA.

## The Bottom Line

For most intrastate carriers, IFTA is not required. The key is to confirm with your state, because a few states do require it, and crossing state lines even once changes your status. Check your routes, vehicle weight, and state rules. If you're unsure, call your state's motor carrier office. It's a five-minute call that can save you from fines and headaches down the road.
