If you're trying to decide between LTL (less-than-truckload) and FTL (full-truckload) shipping, the first thing to understand is how weight drives the choice. LTL is for shipments typically between 150 and 15,000 pounds, while FTL is for anything over that, usually 15,000 pounds or more. But weight isn't the only factor: cost per pound, transit time, and handling requirements matter too. Here's a practical breakdown to help you decide which method works for your freight.

## What Counts as LTL vs FTL Weight?

LTL carriers like FedEx Freight, XPO, and Old Dominion handle freight that doesn't fill an entire trailer. In 2026, the typical LTL weight range is 150 to 15,000 pounds. Shipments under 150 pounds usually go via parcel carriers (UPS, FedEx Ground), not LTL. FTL, on the other hand, is for loads that occupy most or all of a 48- or 53-foot trailer, which usually means 15,000 to 45,000 pounds, depending on the commodity's density.

Here's a quick reference:

| Shipment Weight | Typical Mode | Example Freight |
|----------------|--------------|----------------|
| 1-150 lbs | Parcel | Small boxes, documents |
| 150-15,000 lbs | LTL | Pallets of auto parts, retail goods |
| 15,000-45,000 lbs | FTL | Full pallet loads, machinery, bulk materials |

Note: Some carriers have different cutoffs. For instance, a carrier might accept LTL up to 20,000 pounds if the freight fits on one pallet. Always check with your carrier.

## Cost Comparison: LTL vs FTL

Costs vary widely by lane, weight, and accessorials, but here are realistic 2026 figures:

- **LTL cost per 100 pounds (cwt)**: $25 to $50 for a 1,000-pound shipment, dropping to $15 to $25 for 10,000 pounds. Minimum charges run $75 to $150 per shipment.
- **FTL cost per mile**: $2.50 to $4.50 per mile, depending on fuel, region, and equipment. A 500-mile haul might cost $1,250 to $2,250.
- **Cost per pound**: LTL often costs $0.10 to $0.30 per pound; FTL drops to $0.05 to $0.15 per pound for heavier loads.

**Example**: Shipping 5,000 pounds from Chicago to Dallas (about 800 miles). LTL might cost $600 to $900 (at $12 to $18 per cwt). FTL would cost $2,000 to $3,600 (at $2.50 to $4.50 per mile), but you'd be paying for empty space if you don't fill the trailer.

**When to choose LTL**: If your shipment is under 10,000 pounds and you don't need dedicated equipment, LTL is almost always cheaper.

**When to choose FTL**: If your shipment is over 15,000 pounds, or you need the trailer exclusively (for fragile items, tight schedules, or multiple stops), FTL makes sense.

## Transit Time and Handling

LTL shipments move through hub-and-spoke networks, with freight transferred between trailers at terminals. That adds 1 to 3 days compared to FTL. For example, an 800-mile LTL shipment might take 3 to 5 days; FTL takes 1 to 2 days.

Handling also differs. LTL freight is loaded and unloaded multiple times, increasing the risk of damage. FTL freight is loaded once at origin and unloaded once at destination, so it's safer for fragile or high-value items.

## Practical Steps to Decide This Week

1. **Weigh your shipment exactly**: Use a certified scale. Overestimating weight can push you into FTL when LTL would work; underestimating leads to reweigh fees ($50 to $100).
2. **Measure dimensions**: LTL pricing also depends on cubic feet. If your freight is light but bulky, you might pay for dimensional weight.
3. **Get quotes for both modes**: Use load boards like DAT or Truckstop for FTL spot rates, and get LTL quotes from carriers or a 3PL. Compare total cost, including accessorials like liftgate ($50 to $150) or residential delivery ($75 to $150).
4. **Check your timeline**: If you need it there in 2 days, FTL might be your only option. If you have a week, LTL saves money.
5. **Consider partial truckload (PTL)**: Some carriers offer PTL for 6 to 12 pallets, which is a hybrid between LTL and FTL. It costs more than LTL but less than FTL, and it avoids multiple handling.

## Comparison Table: LTL vs FTL vs PTL

| Feature | LTL | FTL | PTL (Partial Truckload) |
|------------------------|----------------------------|----------------------------|--------------------------|
| Weight range | 150-15,000 lbs | 15,000-45,000 lbs | 5,000-20,000 lbs |

## FAQ

**Q: Can I ship 20,000 pounds via LTL?**  
A: Some carriers accept up to 20,000 pounds, but it's rare. Most cap LTL at 15,000 pounds. Above that, you'll likely need FTL or PTL.

**Q: How does dimensional weight affect LTL pricing?**  
A: LTL carriers use a formula that considers both weight and cubic feet. If your freight is light but large, you'll be charged for the space it takes up. Always measure accurately to avoid surprises.

**Q: Is FTL always faster than LTL?**  
A: Yes, typically. FTL goes direct from origin to destination, while LTL goes through terminals. But if the FTL driver has multiple stops, transit time can increase.

**Q: What if my shipment is under 150 pounds?**  
A: Use parcel carriers like UPS or FedEx. LTL minimums are usually 150 pounds, and you'll pay a high minimum charge for anything less.

## The Bottom Line

The weight of your shipment is the starting point, but not the only factor. For freight under 10,000 pounds, LTL is usually cost-effective. For anything over 15,000 pounds, FTL is the standard. Between 10,000 and 15,000 pounds, get quotes for both, plus PTL, and factor in transit time and handling. Weigh your freight, measure it, and compare total costs. That's how you make the right call every time.
