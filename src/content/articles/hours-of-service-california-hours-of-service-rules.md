California has its own hours of service (HOS) rules that go beyond federal regulations. If you drive a commercial vehicle in California, you must follow both sets of rules. This guide explains the key differences, what they mean for your daily driving, and how to stay compliant.

## Federal vs. California HOS: The Basics

Federal HOS rules, set by the FMCSA, apply nationwide. California adds its own requirements, mainly around meal and rest breaks. The state's rules are stricter in some areas, and they apply to all drivers, not just those based in California.

Here is a quick comparison:

| Rule | Federal | California |
|------|---------|------------|
| Driving limit | 11 hours after 10 consecutive hours off duty | Same as federal |
| On-duty limit | 14 hours after coming on duty | Same as federal |
| 30-minute break | Required after 8 hours of driving | Required, but also meal breaks |
| Meal break | Not required by FMCSA | 30-minute meal break before the end of the 5th hour of work |
| Rest break | Not required by FMCSA | 10-minute rest break for every 4 hours worked |
| Sleeper berth | 10 hours off duty, can be split | Same as federal |

California's meal and rest break rules apply to all employees, including truck drivers. They are enforced by the California Labor Code, not the FMCSA.

## California Meal Break Rules

California law requires a 30-minute meal break for any work shift over 5 hours. The break must be uninterrupted and you must be relieved of all duties. If you work more than 10 hours, you need a second meal break before the end of the 12th hour.

For truck drivers, this means you must plan your day to include these breaks. You cannot skip a meal break to get to your destination faster. If you do, you could face penalties.

Practical steps:
- Schedule your meal break before your 5th hour of work. For example, if you start at 6 AM, take your break by 11 AM.
- Use your ELD to log the break as "off duty" or "sleeper berth" if you are resting. If you are on duty but not driving, log it as "on duty not driving" only if you are not relieved of duties, but that does not count as a meal break.
- If you are an owner-operator, you are not an employee, but the rules still apply if you have employees. If you are a solo driver, you still must comply with the state's meal break rules for your own safety.

## California Rest Break Rules

California also requires a 10-minute rest break for every 4 hours worked, or major fraction thereof. This is in addition to meal breaks. The rest break must be paid, and you must be relieved of all duties.

For a typical 14-hour on-duty day, you would need at least two rest breaks (one after 4 hours, one after 8 hours). You can combine a rest break with a meal break if you take them together, but the rest break must be taken before the meal break if you are working more than 4 hours.

Practical steps:
- Set reminders on your ELD or phone to take a 10-minute break every 4 hours.
- Use the break to stretch, hydrate, and check your equipment. It is not just a legal requirement; it improves alertness.
- Log the rest break as "off duty" if you are fully relieved. If you are on duty but not driving, that does not count.

## How California HOS Affects Your Daily Log

Your ELD must accurately reflect your breaks. When you take a meal or rest break, log it as "off duty" if you are completely free from duties. If you are at a truck stop and can leave your truck, that is off duty. If you are at a shipper and cannot leave, you may be on duty not driving, but that does not satisfy the break requirement.

Important: California does not allow you to use the "personal conveyance" exception to avoid breaks. If you move your truck during a break, it is not a break.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

Violating California's meal and rest break rules can result in penalties. For each violation, you could face a penalty of $50 for the first violation and $100 for each subsequent violation, per the California Labor Code. However, if you are an employee, your employer is responsible for providing breaks. If they do not, they can be sued for damages.

For owner-operators, you are responsible for your own compliance. If you are stopped by the CHP (California Highway Patrol), they can check your logs. If you are out of compliance, you could face fines and out-of-service orders.

## Practical Tips for California Driving

- Plan your route with break stops in mind. Use apps like Trucker Path or DAT to find rest areas and truck stops.
- If you are new to California, be aware that traffic can be heavy, especially around LA and the Bay Area. Factor in delays when planning your 14-hour window.
- Keep a paper log as a backup if your ELD fails, but remember that California requires ELDs for most drivers.
- Check with your dispatcher or employer about their policies on meal breaks. Some companies have specific procedures.

## FAQ

**Q: Do California meal break rules apply to interstate drivers?**
A: Yes, they apply to any driver working in California, regardless of interstate or intrastate operations. The state labor laws cover all employees.

**Q: Can I split my meal break into two 15-minute breaks?**
A: No, California requires a single, uninterrupted 30-minute meal break. You cannot split it.

**Q: What if I am an owner-operator and not an employee?**
A: The meal and rest break rules apply to employees. As an owner-operator, you are not covered by those specific labor code sections, but you still must follow federal HOS. However, if you have employees, you must provide breaks.

**Q: How do I log a meal break on my ELD?**
A: Log it as "off duty" if you are fully relieved. If you are in the sleeper berth, use "sleeper berth." If you are on duty but not driving, that does not count as a break.

## The Bottom Line

California's HOS rules add meal and rest break requirements that are stricter than federal rules. As a driver, you must take a 30-minute meal break before your 5th hour and a 10-minute rest break every 4 hours. Plan your day to include these breaks, log them correctly, and stay compliant. Ignoring these rules can lead to fines and safety risks. Stay informed, because rules can change. Check the California Department of Industrial Relations website for the latest updates. Drive safe.
