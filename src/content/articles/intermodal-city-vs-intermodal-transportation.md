City and intermodal transportation serve different freight needs, and choosing between them depends on your cargo, routes, and schedule. City trucking handles short-haul local deliveries, often with multiple stops per day, while intermodal moves containers between rail ramps, ports, and warehouses, combining truck and rail. This guide breaks down the key differences so you can decide which model fits your operation.

## What Is City Trucking?

City trucking, also called local or P&D (pickup and delivery), covers freight within a metro area or a radius of roughly 50 to 150 miles. Drivers typically run routes that start and end at the same terminal or home base each day. Loads are often LTL (less-than-truckload), meaning you might pick up from several shippers and deliver to several receivers on one route.

Typical equipment includes box trucks, straight trucks, or day cab tractors pulling dry vans or reefers. You are home daily, which is a major lifestyle advantage. Pay often ranges from $20 to $30 per hour for company drivers, or $0.55 to $0.75 per mile for owner-operators, depending on the market and freight type.

## What Is Intermodal Transportation?

Intermodal transportation moves freight in standardized containers using multiple modes: truck, rail, and ship. In the context of trucking, intermodal drivers haul containers to or from rail ramps and ports. The truck portion is called drayage. You might pick up a container at a port, deliver it to a rail ramp, or take one from a ramp to a warehouse.

Intermodal runs are often longer than city routes, ranging from 50 to 300 miles per leg, but the driver is not on the road for days at a time. You are typically home daily or every other day. Equipment is usually a semi-tractor with a chassis that fits standard 20, 40, or 53-foot containers. Pay for owner-operators in drayage often nets $1.00 to $1.50 per mile, but that includes fuel and maintenance. Company drivers might earn $25 to $35 per hour or a percentage of the load.

## Key Differences: City vs Intermodal

The core difference is the freight handling and the schedule. City trucking involves multiple stops, tight windows, and frequent loading and unloading. Intermodal involves one container, one pickup, and one delivery, but you deal with port congestion, chassis availability, and rail schedules.

### Schedule and Time

City routes are predictable: you know your stops and your day ends when the route is done. Intermodal schedules depend on rail arrival times and port operating hours. A train delay can mean waiting at the ramp, which eats into your hours of service. However, intermodal loads often allow for more driving time per day because there are fewer stops.

### Equipment and Maintenance

City trucks often have liftgates and pallet jacks for LTL freight. Intermodal tractors need a chassis that matches the container size, and you may need to inspect the chassis for roadworthiness. Chassis pools are common at ports, but availability can be a headache. Maintenance costs are similar, but intermodal idling at ramps can increase fuel use.

### Revenue and Costs

City trucking offers steady, predictable income with lower per-mile rates but more miles per day. Intermodal offers higher per-mile rates but more deadhead (empty miles) and waiting time. Owner-operators in intermodal also face detention and per diem charges, which can add up if not managed.

## Which One Should You Choose?

Your choice depends on your priorities. If you value predictable hours and being home every night, city trucking is a solid option. If you prefer longer hauls with fewer stops and don't mind occasional waiting, intermodal can be more lucrative.

Consider your experience level. City trucking is easier to break into, especially with a Class B CDL. Intermodal requires a Class A CDL and often a Transportation Worker Identification Credential (TWIC) for port access. You also need to understand chassis inspection and container securement.

## FAQ

**Is intermodal harder on your truck?**

Intermodal involves more highway miles and idling at ramps, which can increase wear on the engine and tires. City driving has more stop-and-go, which wears brakes and clutches faster. Both have their costs, so budget for maintenance accordingly.

**Do you need a special license for intermodal?**

You need a Class A CDL for any semi-truck, including intermodal. For port work, you may need a TWIC card, which requires a background check and costs about $125 for five years. Some rail ramps also require specific training.

**Can you make more money in city or intermodal?**

Owner-operators often earn more per mile in intermodal, but deadhead and waiting can cut into profits. City drivers may have lower rates but more consistent miles. In 2026, a city driver might gross $60,000 to $80,000 annually, while an intermodal driver could gross $70,000 to $100,000, but expenses vary.

**Which has better home time?**

Both offer daily home time, but city routes are more predictable. Intermodal schedules can change due to rail delays, so you might get home later than planned. If strict home time is critical, city is safer.

## The Bottom Line

City and intermodal transportation are both viable paths, but they suit different drivers. City trucking offers routine, multiple stops, and lower entry barriers. Intermodal offers higher per-mile rates and longer hauls, but with more variability. Assess your goals, run the numbers, and talk to drivers in your area. The right choice is the one that matches your lifestyle and financial needs. Start with the steps above, and you'll be on the road with confidence.
