If you're trying to decide between full truckload (FTL) and less-than-truckload (LTL) shipping, the answer comes down to volume, cost, and transit time. FTL means your freight fills an entire trailer, while LTL means you share trailer space with other shippers' cargo. In 2026, FTL rates average $2.00 to $3.50 per mile, while LTL rates are typically $0.10 to $0.25 per pound, with a $75 to $150 minimum. Here's a practical breakdown to help you choose.

## What Is FTL (Full Truckload)?

FTL is when you book an entire trailer for your shipment, whether it's a 53-foot dry van, reefer, or flatbed. You pay for the whole truck, regardless of whether you use all the space. This is ideal for shipments over 10,000 pounds or that take up more than 12 to 15 linear feet of trailer space.

**Pros:**
- Faster transit: direct loading to unloading, no terminal handling.
- Less risk of damage or loss: freight stays in one trailer, handled only at pickup and delivery.
- More control: you can specify loading/unloading times, and the truck is dedicated to you.

**Cons:**
- Higher cost per shipment: you pay for the whole trailer even if it's not full.
- Less flexibility: you need enough freight to justify the cost.

## What Is LTL (Less-Than-Truckload)?

LTL is for shipments that don't fill a trailer, typically 150 to 10,000 pounds. Your freight is consolidated with other shippers' freight in the same trailer. It moves through a network of terminals where freight is transferred between trailers.

**Pros:**
- Lower cost for smaller shipments: you only pay for the space you use.
- More frequent departures: LTL carriers run daily schedules between major hubs.
- Access to a wider network: you can ship to remote areas without paying for a full truck.

**Cons:**
- Longer transit times: freight sits at terminals for sorting and consolidation.
- Higher risk of damage: multiple handling points increase the chance of issues.
- Less control: you have limited influence over pickup and delivery times.

## Cost Comparison: FTL vs LTL in 2026

Here's a realistic cost comparison based on 2026 rates. Remember, these are ranges and vary by lane, fuel surcharges, and carrier.

| Shipment Type | Cost Range | Best For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FTL (full truckload) | $2.00-$3.50 per mile; $500-$1,500 for 500 miles | Shipments over 10,000 lbs or needing dedicated transit |
| LTL (per 100 lbs) | $10-$25 per 100 lbs (CWT) | Shipments 150-10,000 lbs |
| LTL minimum charge | $75-$150 per shipment | Small shipments under 500 lbs |
| LTL with liftgate | Add $50-$100 | Shipments needing liftgate at delivery |
| LTL with residential delivery | Add $50-$150 | Deliveries to homes or businesses without docks |

**Example:** A 1,500-pound shipment from Chicago to Dallas (800 miles):
- FTL: 800 miles at $2.50/mile = $2,000
- LTL: 15 CWT at $15/CWT = $225, plus fuel surcharge and minimums, likely $300-$400 total.

Clearly, LTL is cheaper for small shipments, but FTL becomes cost-effective when you approach a full trailer.

## When to Choose FTL

Choose FTL if:
- Your shipment weighs more than 10,000 pounds or fills more than 12 linear feet of trailer.
- You need expedited or time-sensitive delivery: FTL is direct, so it's often faster.
- Your freight is fragile, high-value, or requires special handling: less handling means less risk.
- You have enough volume to fill a trailer regularly: you can negotiate better rates.

## When to Choose LTL

Choose LTL if:
- Your shipment is under 10,000 pounds and doesn't fill a trailer.
- You want to save money on shipping costs.
- You can tolerate longer transit times (2 to 5 days for regional, 5 to 10 for cross-country).
- You don't need dedicated equipment or special handling.

## Practical Steps to Decide This Week

1. **Weigh and measure your freight.** Get exact weight and dimensions. This is the first step to getting accurate quotes.
2. **Get quotes from multiple carriers.** For FTL, use load boards like DAT or Truckstop.com. For LTL, get quotes from carriers like FedEx Freight, Old Dominion, or XPO. Online freight marketplaces can also give instant estimates.
3. **Calculate your cost per mile or per pound.** Compare the total cost of FTL vs LTL for your specific shipment. Don't forget fuel surcharges, accessorials, and insurance.
4. **Factor in transit time.** If your customer needs it fast, FTL might be worth the extra cost. If not, LTL can save money.
5. **Consider your freight class.** LTL rates depend on freight class (based on density, stowability, handling, and liability). Know your class to avoid surprises.

## FAQ

**Is FTL always faster than LTL?**
Yes, generally. FTL is direct from pickup to delivery, while LTL involves terminal handling and consolidation, which adds 1 to 3 days in transit.

**Can I use LTL for a full trailer?**
Technically, no. If you have enough freight to fill a trailer, it's more cost-effective to use FTL. LTL carriers will charge you for the space, but you'll pay a premium for the service.

**How do I get the best LTL rate?**
Negotiate with carriers, especially if you ship regularly. Use a freight broker or online marketplace to compare rates. Also, ensure your freight class is correct to avoid reclassification fees.

**What about freight insurance?**
Both FTL and LTL carriers offer limited liability, but it's often insufficient. Consider buying additional cargo insurance, especially for high-value shipments. Costs range from $0.50 to $2.00 per $100 of declared value.

## The Bottom Line

The choice between FTL and LTL comes down to volume and urgency. If you have a full trailer or need fast, direct delivery, FTL is the way to go. If your shipment is small and you can wait, LTL will save you money. Always get multiple quotes and factor in all costs, not just the base rate. As a trucker, understanding these options helps you serve your customers better and maximize your revenue, whether you're hauling a full load or consolidating freight.

For owner-operators, knowing when to recommend LTL to a customer can also open up new business opportunities, even if you don't haul LTL yourself. And if you do run LTL, you'll need a solid understanding of freight classes and terminal operations. Either way, the key is to match the service to the shipment's needs.
