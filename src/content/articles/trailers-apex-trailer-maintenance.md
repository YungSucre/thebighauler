Trailer maintenance is not glamorous, but it is where small problems become big ones if ignored. A well-maintained trailer keeps you legal, safe, and profitable. This guide covers the essentials: what to check, how often, and what it costs. You can start with a simple walk-around today and build a routine that prevents breakdowns and DOT violations.

## Why Trailer Maintenance Matters

Your trailer takes the same abuse as your truck, but it gets less attention. A failed brake or a blown tire can cost you thousands in repairs, downtime, and fines. According to the FMCSA, brake violations are among the top out-of-service criteria. Regular maintenance is not optional; it is a business expense that pays off. A basic inspection routine takes 30 minutes and can catch issues before they strand you on the shoulder.

## The 30-Minute Walk-Around: What to Check Weekly

Do this every week, or before every long trip if you run irregularly. Use a flashlight and a tire gauge. Look for:

- **Tires**: Check pressure when cold. Proper pressure is usually 100-110 psi for trailer tires, but confirm the sidewall rating. Look for cuts, bulges, or uneven wear. Use a tread depth gauge; replace at 4/32 inch for steer, 2/32 for drive and trailer, but many fleets replace at 4/32 for all positions.
- **Brakes**: Check air pressure builds to 100-120 psi. Listen for air leaks. Inspect brake chambers for rust or damage. Look at the slack adjuster: it should not have excessive travel. If you have drum brakes, check for oil or grease on the lining.
- **Lights and reflectors**: Test all lights: turn signals, brake, clearance, and marker. Replace burned bulbs immediately. Clean reflectors; they are cheap and critical for visibility.
- **Coupling and landing gear**: Check the kingpin for wear and the fifth wheel plate for lubrication. Lower the landing gear and inspect the legs for cracks. Ensure the crank handle is secure.
- **Suspension and frame**: Look for cracks, loose U-bolts, or missing hardware. Check the air bags (if equipped) for leaks and proper inflation.
- **Doors and seals**: Open and close all doors. Check the rubber seals for tears. A bad seal lets in water, which ruins cargo and causes rust.

## Brake Maintenance: The Most Critical System

Brakes are your number one safety system. Federal law requires brakes on all axles, and they must work within specific limits. Here is what to do:

- **Check brake adjustment** every week. Use a ruler or a brake stroke indicator. The pushrod travel should be within the range marked on the chamber (usually 1.5 to 2 inches for a standard type 30 chamber). If it exceeds that, have it adjusted.
- **Inspect brake linings** every 30,000 miles or 6 months, whichever comes first. Replace linings when they are worn to 1/4 inch or less. Cost: $300-$600 per axle for parts and labor, depending on whether you do it yourself.
- **Check brake drums** for heat cracks, scoring, or out-of-round. A drum can be turned once, but if it is too thin, replace it. New drums run $100-$200 each.
- **Test the emergency brake** by releasing the trailer brakes and pulling the red knob. The trailer should stop within a reasonable distance.

## Tire Care: Extend Life and Avoid Blowouts

Tires are the most common cause of roadside breakdowns. Proper maintenance can double their life.

- **Check pressure** weekly, not just visually. Use a calibrated gauge. Underinflation by 10 psi can reduce tread life by 10% and increase fuel consumption.
- **Inspect for damage** after every trip. Look for sidewall cuts, bulges, or nails. Remove stones from treads.
- **Rotate tires** every 20,000-25,000 miles. This evens out wear, especially on trailers that are loaded unevenly.
- **Replace tires** when tread depth is below 2/32 inch, or if there is any belt separation. A new trailer tire costs $200-$400 each. Consider retreads for trailers; they are common and safe if done by a reputable shop.

## Wheel Bearings and Hubs: The Silent Killer

Bearing failure can cause a wheel to seize, which is dangerous and expensive. Here is the routine:

- **Check for heat** after a long drive. Touch the hub (carefully) or use an infrared thermometer. If one hub is significantly hotter than others, the bearing is failing.
- **Repack bearings** every 12 months or 100,000 miles. This involves removing the hub, cleaning the old grease, and packing new grease. Cost: $150-$300 per axle if done by a shop.
- **Inspect seals** for leaks. Grease on the inside of the tire or on the drum is a sign of a bad seal.

## Electrical and Lighting: Keep It Legal

DOT requires all lights to work. A burned-out marker light is a ticket magnet.

- **Check the 7-way plug** for corrosion or bent pins. Clean with electrical contact cleaner.
- **Test all lights** with a helper or a tester. Replace bulbs as needed. LED lights last longer but cost more upfront.
- **Check the wiring** for chafing or exposed wires, especially near the frame and under the trailer.

## Trailer Frame and Body: Rust and Corrosion

Rust is not just ugly; it weakens the structure. Address it early.

- **Inspect the frame** for rust, especially around welds and crossmembers. Use a hammer to tap for weak spots. If you find flaking rust, scrape and treat it with a rust converter.
- **Check the floor** for rot or damage. A soft spot can lead to cargo falling through.
- **Seal any cracks** in the roof or walls. Use a quality sealant designed for trailers.

## Seasonal and Annual Maintenance Schedule

Here is a simple schedule to keep you on track:

## FAQ

**How often should I grease the trailer's moving parts?**

Grease the fifth wheel plate, landing gear, and door hinges every month or every 10,000 miles. Use a lithium-based grease. Over-greasing can attract dirt, so wipe off excess.

**Can I do trailer maintenance myself, or should I hire a shop?**

You can do basic checks and tire pressure yourself. Brake work and bearing repacks require tools and experience. If you are not comfortable, hire a certified mechanic. It is cheaper than a breakdown.

**What is the most common DOT violation on trailers?**

Brake-related violations, followed by lighting and tire issues. Keeping a log of your inspections can help you prove due diligence.

**How do I know if my trailer brakes are out of adjustment?**

Listen for a grinding sound when braking, or check the pushrod travel. If it exceeds the limit, have them adjusted. Some trailers have automatic slack adjusters, but they still need manual checks.

## The Bottom Line

Trailer maintenance is a non-negotiable part of your business. A weekly 30-minute inspection, a monthly grease job, and an annual professional check will keep you safe and legal. The cost of maintenance is a fraction of the cost of a breakdown, a fine, or an accident. Start today: do a walk-around, check your tire pressures, and look at your brakes. Your trailer will thank you, and so will your bottom line.
