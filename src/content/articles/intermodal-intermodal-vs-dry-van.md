If you're trying to decide between intermodal and dry van, you're weighing two very different ways to run a truck. Intermodal means hauling shipping containers between rail ramps, ports, and customer facilities. Dry van means hauling standard 53-foot trailers, usually from warehouse to warehouse. Both can make money, but they differ in pay, home time, equipment wear, and daily headaches. Here's the straight comparison so you can pick the right lane for your goals.

## The Core Differences

Intermodal work revolves around container chassis and rail schedules. You pick up a container at a rail ramp or port, deliver it locally, then grab another one. Dry van work is simpler: you hook to a 53-foot box trailer, drive to a shipper, load, drive to the receiver, unload, and repeat. Intermodal is more regional and predictable, while dry van often means longer over-the-road trips and more time away from home.

## Pay and Income Potential

In 2026, intermodal drivers typically earn $55,000 to $75,000 per year as company drivers, with experienced owner-operators grossing $120,000 to $180,000 before expenses. Dry van company drivers average $50,000 to $70,000, and owner-operators gross $100,000 to $150,000. Intermodal often pays per move or per diem, and you might get paid by the load rather than by the mile, which can be better in congested areas. Dry van is almost always per mile, averaging $0.50 to $0.70 per mile for company drivers and $1.50 to $2.20 per mile for owner-operators before fuel and maintenance.

## Home Time and Lifestyle

Intermodal is a local or regional gig. You're usually home every day or every few days, especially if you live near a major rail hub like Chicago, Los Angeles, or Dallas. You'll deal with port congestion and rail delays, but you'll sleep in your own bed most nights. Dry van over-the-road means weeks on the road, living in the truck, and dealing with shippers and receivers who might keep you waiting. If you value being home, intermodal wins. If you don't mind long stretches away and want to see the country, dry van OTR might suit you.

## Equipment and Maintenance

Intermodal uses a container chassis, which is a simple frame with wheels and a locking mechanism. Chassis maintenance is minimal, but you'll deal with chassis pools, roadability issues, and container damage. You don't own the container, so you're not responsible for its condition, but you must inspect it. Dry van means you own or lease the trailer, which costs $30,000 to $50,000 new. You're responsible for tires, brakes, and trailer repairs. In intermodal, the rail company or chassis pool often handles major repairs, but you still need to do pre-trips and report issues.

## Regulations and Paperwork

Both require a CDL and compliance with hours of service. Intermodal often involves more port and rail yard paperwork, including chassis inspection reports and container seals. You'll also deal with TWIC (Transportation Worker Identification Credential) if you go into ports, which costs about $125 and requires a background check. Dry van paperwork is simpler: bill of lading, proof of delivery, and maybe a lumped or detention form. Intermodal runs are often shorter, so you may have more stops per day, which means more log entries and inspection reports.

## Pros and Cons

**Intermodal Pros:**
- More home time
- Predictable routes
- Less deadhead (empty miles)
- Lower trailer maintenance costs

**Intermodal Cons:**
- Port and rail delays
- Chassis availability issues
- More stop-and-go driving
- Can be physically demanding (twisting twistlocks, checking containers)

**Dry Van Pros:**
- Simpler loading and unloading
- More variety in destinations
- Higher per-mile rates for long hauls
- Easier to find loads on load boards

**Dry Van Cons:**
- Long time away from home
- Higher trailer maintenance costs
- More deadhead if you're not careful
- Shipper/receiver delays can eat into your hours

## How to Choose: Practical Steps

1. **Assess your lifestyle needs.** If you need to be home daily or weekly, intermodal is the better fit. If you're okay with being out for weeks, dry van OTR is viable.
2. **Check your local market.** Look at job boards and load boards in your area. If you live near a major rail ramp or port, intermodal jobs are plentiful. If you're in a rural area, dry van might be the only option.
3. **Calculate startup costs.** If you're an owner-operator, compare the cost of a used truck plus chassis versus a used truck plus dry van trailer. Include insurance, which runs $8,000 to $15,000 per year for either.
4. **Talk to drivers.** Hit up truck stops or online forums. Ask intermodal drivers about chassis availability and rail delays. Ask dry van drivers about detention pay and home time.
5. **Try a short-term lease or rental.** Some companies offer lease-purchase or rental programs. You can test intermodal for a month, then switch if it's not for you.

## FAQ

**Is intermodal harder on your truck?**
Not necessarily. Intermodal involves more stop-and-go and idling at rail yards, which can increase fuel use and wear on brakes. But you're driving fewer miles, so engine wear is lower overall.

**Can you make more money with intermodal?**
Often yes, because you're paid per move and you can do more moves in a day. But it depends on your area and the freight volumes. In busy ports, intermodal can out-earn dry van by 10-20%.

**Do you need a special endorsement for intermodal?**
No, a standard Class A CDL is enough. But you may need a TWIC card for port access, and some rail yards require a background check.

**What's the biggest downside of dry van?**
Time away from home. Many dry van drivers are out 2-3 weeks at a time, which strains relationships and can lead to burnout. If you're okay with that, dry van offers more route variety.

## The Bottom Line

Intermodal and dry van are both solid careers, but they serve different drivers. Intermodal gives you home time and predictable work, but you deal with rail and port congestion. Dry van offers variety and long-haul pay, but you sacrifice being home. Look at your priorities: if family time matters most, go intermodal. If you want to maximize income and don't mind the road, dry van might be your lane. Either way, start by checking local job availability and talking to drivers who do the work. That's the best way to make the right call for 2026.
