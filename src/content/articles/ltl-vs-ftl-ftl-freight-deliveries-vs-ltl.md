If you're new to trucking, you've probably seen the terms FTL and LTL thrown around. FTL means Full Truckload, where your freight takes up the entire trailer. LTL means Less Than Truckload, where your freight shares trailer space with other shippers' cargo. The choice between them affects your cost, transit time, and how you handle freight. Here's the straight answer: FTL is cheaper per pound and faster, but you pay for the whole truck. LTL is cheaper for small shipments, but you pay for space and wait for consolidation. Below, we break down the differences so you can decide which fits your operation.

## What Is FTL Freight?

FTL, or Full Truckload, is when your shipment fills an entire trailer, either by volume or weight. You're essentially renting the whole truck, so you get direct, non-stop service from pickup to delivery. This is ideal for shipments over 10,000 pounds or that take up more than 10 linear feet of trailer space.

**Key characteristics:**
- You pay for the entire trailer, not just the space you use.
- Direct route: no stops at terminals for consolidation.
- Faster transit times, often 1-3 days for regional hauls.
- Less handling: freight is loaded once and unloaded once, reducing damage risk.

**Cost range (2026):** For a standard 53-foot dry van, expect to pay $1.50 to $3.00 per mile, depending on lane and season. A typical 1,000-mile haul runs $1,500 to $3,000. For specialized equipment like reefers or flatbeds, add 20-30%.

## What Is LTL Freight?

LTL, or Less Than Truckload, is for shipments that don't fill a trailer. Your freight is consolidated with other shippers' cargo at a terminal, then loaded onto a trailer for linehaul. This means multiple stops and more handling, but you only pay for the space you use.

**Key characteristics:**
- You pay based on weight, dimensions, and freight class.
- Transit times are longer, typically 3-7 days for regional, 5-10 days for cross-country.
- Freight is handled multiple times (pickup, terminal, linehaul, delivery), increasing damage risk.
- Accessorial fees can add up: liftgate, residential delivery, inside delivery, etc.

**Cost range (2026):** LTL rates average $0.10 to $0.30 per pound, but vary widely by class and lane. A 500-pound shipment might cost $100 to $300, while a 5,000-pound shipment could run $500 to $1,500. Minimum charges are common, often $75 to $150 per shipment.

## FTL vs LTL: Key Differences

| Factor | FTL | LTL |
|--------|-----|-----|
| **Cost** | Per mile, $1.50-$3.00/mile | Per pound/class, $0.10-$0.30/lb |
| **Transit time** | 1-3 days regional | 3-7 days regional |
| **Handling** | Minimal (load once, unload once) | Multiple touches at terminals |
| **Damage risk** | Low | Higher due to handling |
| **Best for** | Shipments >10,000 lbs or >10 linear ft | Shipments <10,000 lbs or <10 linear ft |
| **Booking** | Direct with carrier or broker | Through LTL carrier or broker |
| **Tracking** | Real-time GPS often included | Terminal scans, less frequent updates |

## When to Choose FTL

Choose FTL when:
- Your shipment weighs more than 10,000 pounds.
- You need expedited delivery (e.g., just-in-time inventory).
- Your freight is fragile or high-value, and you want minimal handling.
- You're shipping a full pallet or multiple pallets that take up most of the trailer.
- You want to avoid accessorial fees and complex class-based pricing.

**Practical step:** Get quotes from at least three carriers or brokers. Use load boards like DAT or Truckstop to compare spot rates. If you're a carrier, FTL is your bread and butter; focus on building direct relationships with shippers to avoid broker fees.

## When to Choose LTL

Choose LTL when:
- Your shipment is under 10,000 pounds or less than 10 linear feet.
- You're shipping less-than-pallet quantities (e.g., a few boxes).
- You're willing to trade speed for cost savings.
- You can package your freight to withstand multiple handlings.
- You're shipping to residential or remote areas where FTL would be overkill.

**Practical step:** For LTL, always get a class rating from your carrier or use a freight class calculator. Factor in accessorials: liftgate ($50-$100), residential delivery ($50-$150), inside delivery ($50-$200), and appointment fees. Compare total cost, not just base rate.

## How to Decide: Step-by-Step

1. **Weigh and measure your freight.** Get exact weight and dimensions. If it's over 10,000 lbs or more than 10 linear feet, FTL is likely better.
2. **Calculate your budget.** If you're shipping under 5,000 lbs, LTL is almost always cheaper. For 5,000-10,000 lbs, compare quotes.
3. **Check transit time needs.** If you need it there in 2 days, FTL is safer. If 5 days works, LTL is fine.
4. **Assess freight value and fragility.** High-value or fragile items go FTL to reduce damage risk.
5. **Get quotes.** Use a broker or direct carrier for FTL, and an LTL carrier like FedEx Freight or XPO for LTL. Compare total costs including accessorials.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use LTL for a full pallet?**
A: Yes, if the pallet is under 10 linear feet and under 10,000 lbs. Many LTL carriers accept single pallets. Just ensure it's palletized and shrink-wrapped.

**Q: Is FTL always faster?**
A: Generally yes, because there are no terminal stops. But if the FTL carrier has to wait for a backhaul, it could delay. LTL has scheduled departures, so transit times are predictable.

**Q: How do freight classes affect LTL cost?**
A: Freight class (50-500) is based on density, stowability, handling, and liability. Higher class means higher cost per pound. For example, Class 100 (density 4-6 lbs/cubic ft) costs more than Class 50 (density >50 lbs/cubic ft).

**Q: What if my shipment is exactly at the FTL/LTL threshold?**
A: At 10,000 lbs or 10 linear feet, get quotes for both. Sometimes LTL is cheaper if you have a low class, but FTL offers faster transit and less handling. Weigh the trade-offs.

## The Bottom Line

FTL and LTL serve different needs. FTL is for big, heavy, or urgent shipments where you want speed and minimal handling. LTL is for smaller, lighter freight where cost per pound matters more than speed. As a driver or small fleet owner, knowing which to use can save you thousands annually. Start by analyzing your typical shipment sizes and lanes. For FTL, build relationships with brokers or shippers directly. For LTL, get a freight class guide and always factor in accessorials. Test both on a few shipments to see which fits your operation. The right choice depends on your freight, budget, and timeline, so run the numbers before you book.
